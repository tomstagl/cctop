Why is thinking red?
Because I picked five colours that were easy to tell apart and stopped thinking. That is the whole reason — and it is a bug, not a scheme. Here is what it costs, and what the bar should say instead.
the rule it breaks
cctop already has a colour language, declared once in themes/*.toml and used on every other row: ok = fine, warn = a threshold crossed, crit = act now, accent = a key you can press. The context bar spends those same four on categories — which slice is which — four rows away from where they mean state. A status colour used as a series colour is the one thing every visualization guide forbids, and this is why: the reader has to hold two contradictory dictionaries at once.

One hue. Lightness carries the order, and the order is how much you can do about it. The dim end is fixed cost; the bright end is the part you can move this afternoon. No second dictionary, and it still reads with the colour off — the segments alternate ▇ and ▆, which stacked_bar() already does.

Five slices sit at ΔE 5.4–9.9 whatever hue you choose — too close for colour alone. Three slices clear the ≥ 15 bar on five of the six bundled themes, and default-light lands at 13.7, inside the band where the alternating glyph makes it legal. So the overview carries three and panel 1 keeps all five, where every slice has its own labelled row and identity never rides on colour at all.

the learning, from the other side of the screen
The context bar is not a pie chart of what happened. It is a list of levers, and four of the five are bolted down.

Today the colour says the opposite of all that. The biggest slice you control is green — which four rows up means nothing to do — and the slice you can do nothing about is red, which four rows up means act now. Someone who learns the dashboard's colours from the meters then reads this bar backwards.
